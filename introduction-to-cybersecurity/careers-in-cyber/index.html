    Careers in Cyber¶

    A comprehensive A–Z guide to understanding, entering, and growing in the cybersecurity profession.

    1. Why Cybersecurity?¶

    Cybersecurity is one of the fastest-growing and most in-demand fields in the world. As digital transformation accelerates, organizations of every size face a relentless wave of threats — from ransomware and phishing to nation-state espionage and insider threats.

    Key Stats (as of 2025)¶

    • 3.5 million+ unfilled cybersecurity jobs globally (ISC² estimates)
    • Average salary for a security professional: $100,000–$160,000 USD in the US
    • Every industry needs security — finance, healthcare, government, education, retail
    • Cyber attacks cost the global economy over $8 trillion per year
    • The field is recession-resistant — security is always a necessity

    Who Should Consider Cybersecurity?¶

    • People who love problem-solving and puzzles
    • Those interested in technology, networks, and how things break
    • People who care about privacy, ethics, and protecting others
    • Career changers from IT, software development, law enforcement, military, or even psychology

    2. Career Domains Overview¶

    Cybersecurity is not a single job — it's an ecosystem of specializations. Broadly, they fall into:

    Offensive Security (Red Team)¶

    Simulating attackers to find weaknesses before real attackers do. - Penetration Testing - Red Teaming - Exploit Development - Social Engineering

    Defensive Security (Blue Team)¶

    Detecting, responding to, and preventing attacks. - Security Operations (SOC) - Incident Response - Threat Hunting - Digital Forensics

    Purple Team¶

    Bridging offense and defense — offensive teams share findings directly with defenders to improve detection.

    Governance, Risk & Compliance (GRC)¶

    Ensuring organizations follow policies, laws, and industry standards. - Risk Management - Compliance & Auditing - Security Policy Writing - Privacy & Data Protection

    Security Engineering & Architecture¶

    Designing and building secure systems. - Cloud Security - Application Security (AppSec) - Network Security Engineering - Identity & Access Management (IAM)

    Research & Intelligence¶

    Discovering new vulnerabilities and tracking threat actors. - Malware Analysis - Vulnerability Research - Threat Intelligence - Reverse Engineering

    4. Skills & Knowledge Areas¶

    Foundational Technical Skills¶

    • Networking — TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP/S, OSI model, routing, subnetting
    • Operating Systems — Linux (essential), Windows, macOS internals
    • Programming & Scripting — Python, Bash, PowerShell (at minimum)
    • Web Technologies — HTTP, HTML, JavaScript, APIs, cookies, sessions
    • Databases — SQL, NoSQL basics; SQL injection concepts
    • Virtualization — VMware, VirtualBox, containers (Docker)
    • Cloud — AWS/Azure/GCP fundamentals

    Security-Specific Knowledge¶

    • Cryptography — AES, RSA, hashing, PKI, TLS/SSL
    • Authentication — OAuth, SAML, MFA, Kerberos, LDAP
    • Vulnerability Management — CVE, CVSS scoring, patch management
    • SIEM & Log Analysis — Splunk, ELK stack, log correlation
    • Firewalls & IDS/IPS — rule creation, traffic analysis
    • Malware Concepts — RATs, ransomware, rootkits, C2 infrastructure
    • MITRE ATT&CK Framework — adversary TTPs catalog
    • OWASP Top 10 — web application vulnerabilities
    • NIST / ISO 27001 — security frameworks and standards

    Offensive Skills¶

    • Reconnaissance (passive/active)
    • Exploitation techniques
    • Post-exploitation and lateral movement
    • Password cracking and privilege escalation
    • Social engineering and phishing

    Defensive Skills¶

    • Log analysis and SIEM correlation
    • Alert triage and investigation
    • Threat intelligence integration
    • Incident response procedures
    • Endpoint detection (EDR tools)
    • Network traffic analysis (Wireshark, Zeek)

    Tip: Certifications are valuable, but practical skills matter more. Combine certs with hands-on labs and projects.

    6. Degrees & Formal Education¶

    Relevant Degrees¶

    • B.Sc. in Cybersecurity — purpose-built programs covering all domains
    • B.Sc. in Computer Science — strong foundation, widely respected
    • B.Sc. in Information Technology — practical IT focus
    • B.Sc. in Electrical/Computer Engineering — hardware and systems focus
    • M.Sc. in Cybersecurity / Information Security — for specialization
    • MBA with Cyber focus — for leadership/CISO track

    Is a Degree Required?¶

    No. Many successful cybersecurity professionals are self-taught or came from non-CS backgrounds. However: - Degrees help with government and cleared positions (which often require degrees) - Degrees can accelerate entry into larger enterprises - Certifications + labs + portfolio can substitute effectively in most private sector roles

    Alternative Education Paths¶

    • Bootcamps (SANS, TCM Security, Cybrary, SANS Cyber Aces)
    • Community college associates programs
    • Military/government training programs
    • Online degree programs (WGU, SNHU, Arizona State)

    8. Labs & Hands-On Practice¶

    Hands-on practice is the single most important thing you can do to build skills.

    Home Lab Setup¶

    Recommended home lab stack:
- Host machine with 16GB+ RAM
- Hypervisor: VirtualBox (free) or VMware Workstation
- VMs: Kali Linux, Parrot OS, Ubuntu, Windows Server, Windows 10
- Network: Internal-only host-only or NAT network
- Vulnerable VMs: Metasploitable2/3, DVWA, VulnHub machines

    Vulnerable Applications¶

    Resource Purpose
    DVWA (Damn Vulnerable Web App) Web vulnerability practice
    WebGoat OWASP web security learning
    Metasploitable 2/3 Linux/Windows exploitation
    VulnHub Downloadable vulnerable VMs
    HackTheBox Machines Realistic lab targets
    TryHackMe Rooms Guided guided environments
    FLARE-VM Malware analysis workstation
    DetectionLab Blue team / SIEM environment
    PentestLab Web and app pentesting

    Cloud Practice¶

    • AWS Free Tier — Build and attack your own cloud environments
    • CloudGoat (Rhino Security Labs) — Intentionally vulnerable AWS
    • AzureGoat / GCP Goat — Azure/GCP equivalents
    • Kubernetes Goat — Container security practice

    9. Career Pathways¶

    Path 1: SOC Analyst → Incident Responder → Threat Hunter¶

    Entry:   Help Desk / IT Support → CompTIA A+, Network+, Security+
↓
Junior:  SOC Analyst Tier 1 → Learn SIEM (Splunk/ELK), alert triage
↓
Mid:     SOC Analyst Tier 2/3, Incident Responder → GCIH, CySA+
↓
Senior:  Threat Hunter, IR Lead → GCFA, GCIA
↓
Expert:  SOC Manager, Director of IR

    Path 2: IT → Penetration Tester¶

    Entry:   Sysadmin/Network Admin → Networking & Linux fundamentals
↓
Learning: THM, HTB, CEH, eJPT → Python scripting
↓
Junior:  Junior Penetration Tester → PNPT or CompTIA PenTest+
↓
Mid:     Penetration Tester → OSCP
↓
Senior:  Senior Penetration Tester → OSCE, specialize (web/AD/mobile)
↓
Expert:  Red Team Lead, Security Researcher

    Path 3: Developer → Application Security¶

    Entry:   Software Developer → Learn OWASP Top 10, secure coding
↓
Transition: Bug bounty hunting, code review skills
↓
Junior:  AppSec Engineer → GWEB, GWAPT
↓
Senior:  Senior AppSec Engineer, DevSecOps Architect
↓
Expert:  AppSec Lead, Security Architect

    Path 4: GRC & Compliance¶

    Entry:   Business/Law/IT background → CompTIA Security+, ISO 27001 Intro
↓
Junior:  GRC Analyst / Risk Analyst → CISA, ISO 27001 Lead Auditor
↓
Senior:  Senior GRC Analyst, Compliance Manager → CISSP, CISM
↓
Expert:  CISO, VP of Risk, DPO

    Path 5: Research & Vulnerability Discovery¶

    Entry:   Strong CS/programming background
↓
Learning: Reverse engineering (Ghidra, IDA), exploit dev basics
↓
Intermediate: Bug bounty, CVE submissions, GREM
↓
Advanced: Malware analyst, Vulnerability researcher, Zero-day researcher
↓
Expert:  Principal Researcher, Threat Intel Lead

    13. Programming & Scripting¶

    You do not need to be a developer, but coding skills will set you apart.

    Essential Languages¶

    Python — The #1 language in security 

    # Example: Simple port scanner
import socket
for port in range(1, 1025):
    s = socket.socket()
    s.settimeout(0.5)
    if s.connect_ex(('192.168.1.1', port)) == 0:
        print(f"Port {port} is open")
    s.close()

    Bash — Linux automation and scripting 

    # Example: Find SUID binaries
find / -perm -4000 -type f 2>/dev/null

    PowerShell — Windows administration and red team 

    # Example: List running processes
Get-Process | Where-Object {$_.CPU -gt 100} | Select-Object Name, CPU

    Useful Additional Languages¶

    • JavaScript — For XSS, web application testing
    • SQL — For SQLi testing and log queries
    • C/C++ — For exploit development and low-level research
    • Assembly — For reverse engineering
    • Go / Rust — Modern offensive tooling (malware authors use these)
    • Ruby — Legacy (Metasploit modules)

    What to Build¶

    • Port scanners, directory brute-forcers
    • Log parsers and alerting scripts
    • Phishing page templates (for authorized tests)
    • Automation scripts for repetitive recon tasks
    • Custom Metasploit modules

    14. Soft Skills¶

    Technical skills get you the interview. Soft skills get you hired and promoted.

    Communication¶

    • Report writing — Turning technical findings into clear, actionable reports for non-technical audiences
    • Verbal briefings — Presenting findings to executives, clients, and boards
    • Documentation — Writing runbooks, playbooks, and procedures

    Critical Thinking¶

    • Thinking like an attacker (adversarial mindset)
    • Connecting dots across disparate data
    • Prioritizing risk and effort appropriately

    Collaboration¶

    • Working with developers (AppSec requires this constantly)
    • Cross-functional incident response
    • Vendor and third-party management

    Ethics & Integrity¶

    • Handling sensitive data responsibly
    • Disclosing vulnerabilities appropriately
    • Maintaining trust with clients and employers

    Continuous Learning Mindset¶

    • The threat landscape changes constantly
    • Embrace new tools and techniques
    • Stay curious — break things to understand them

    15. Job Hunting & Networking¶

    Where to Find Jobs¶

    • LinkedIn — Most corporate security jobs are posted here
    • Indeed / Glassdoor — Broad job board coverage
    • ClearedJobs.net — US cleared/government roles
    • USAJobs.gov — US federal government positions
    • Dice — Tech-focused job board
    • ISACA / ISC² Job Boards — Association-specific listings
    • InfoSec Jobs (infosec-jobs.com) — Specialized cyber job board
    • SANS Job Board — Community postings
    • Remote.co / We Work Remotely — Remote-friendly security roles

    Networking¶

    • Twitter/X / Infosec.exchange (Mastodon) — Active security community online
    • LinkedIn — Connect with practitioners, engage with posts
    • DEF CON / Black Hat / BSides — Major conferences (in-person + talks)
    • Local BSides events — Affordable, community-driven, great for juniors
    • OWASP local chapters — Web security community
    • ISACA / ISC² local chapters — Professional associations
    • Discord servers — TryHackMe, HTB, TCM Security, InfoSec communities

    Resume Tips¶

    • Tailor your resume to the job description — use their keywords
    • Lead with a strong summary highlighting your specialization
    • Quantify achievements where possible ("Reduced alert backlog by 40%")
    • Include certifications prominently
    • List your home lab and personal projects
    • Include GitHub profile, blog, or CTF profiles (HTB/THM)
    • Keep it to 1–2 pages maximum

    Interview Preparation¶

    • Understand the role's specific focus (red vs blue vs GRC)
    • Be ready to explain concepts out loud (OWASP, CIA triad, TCP handshake)
    • Practice common scenario questions: "Walk me through how you'd investigate a phishing email"
    • Do a technical walkthrough of a CTF machine or past project
    • Research the company's tech stack and industry threats

    16. Building a Portfolio¶

    A portfolio proves you can do the work, even before your first job.

    What to Include¶

    1. CTF write-ups — Document your methodology and findings from machines you've solved
    2. Home lab documentation — Set up a SIEM, document your environment
    3. Vulnerability research — Even on intentionally vulnerable apps
    4. Tools you've built — Scripts, automation, custom tools
    5. Bug bounty findings — Even low-severity submissions count
    6. Blog posts / write-ups — Explaining concepts demonstrates understanding
    7. GitHub repositories — Clean, commented code with READMEs

    Platforms to Showcase Work¶

    • GitHub — Code and scripts
    • Personal blog — Medium, Substack, self-hosted WordPress
    • HackTheBox / TryHackMe profiles — Show your progress
    • LinkedIn — Link all of the above
    • Twitter/X — Engage with the community and share content

    Example Portfolio Projects¶

    • Build a Python-based network scanner
    • Set up a home SOC with ELK or Splunk
    • Perform a full pentest on DVWA and write a professional report
    • Create a phishing simulation and document the campaign
    • Analyze a malware sample and write an analysis report

    17. CTF (Capture the Flag)¶

    CTFs are hacking competitions where you solve challenges to find hidden "flags." They're one of the best ways to develop and demonstrate skills.

    Types of CTF Challenges¶

    • Web — XSS, SQLi, SSRF, XXE, authentication bypass
    • Cryptography — Break ciphers, analyze weak implementations
    • Reverse Engineering — Analyze binaries, find flags in compiled code
    • Binary Exploitation (Pwn) — Buffer overflows, ROP chains
    • Forensics — Analyze packet captures, images, memory dumps
    • OSINT — Find information using public sources
    • Steganography — Find hidden data in files/images
    • Miscellaneous — Programming challenges, puzzles

    Where to Play CTFs¶

    Platform Style
    HackTheBox Machine-based (always active)
    TryHackMe Guided rooms + challenges
    PicoCTF Beginner-friendly, education-focused
    CTFtime.org Calendar of all active CTF competitions
    OverTheWire War game style, Linux focus
    pwn.college Binary exploitation and pwn
    root-me.org Wide variety of challenges

    Tips for CTFs¶

    • Document everything — screenshots and notes as you go
    • Use walkthroughs after you've tried — don't spoil yourself too early
    • Specialize before going broad
    • Team up for competitions
    • Write up your solutions — even failed attempts

    18. Bug Bounty Programs¶

    Bug bounty programs pay researchers to find and responsibly disclose vulnerabilities in their systems.

    How It Works¶

    1. Register on a bug bounty platform
    2. Read the program's scope carefully (what's in/out of scope)
    3. Recon the target (within scope)
    4. Find and verify a vulnerability
    5. Write a clear, reproducible report
    6. Submit and wait for triage
    7. Receive payment (if valid and in scope)

    Platforms¶

    Platform Notable Programs
    HackerOne Uber, Twitter, DoD, PayPal
    Bugcrowd Tesla, Mastercard, OpenAI
    Intigriti European-focused companies
    Synack Vetted/private programs
    YesWeHack European platform
    Open Bug Bounty Coordinated disclosure

    Payout Ranges (approx.)¶

    Severity Typical Range
    Informational $0–100
    Low $50–500
    Medium $200–2,500
    High $1,000–15,000
    Critical $5,000–$100,000+

    Getting Started in Bug Bounty¶

    • Start with PortSwigger Web Security Academy — free labs covering all OWASP
    • Learn Burp Suite inside out
    • Begin with programs that have wide scope (VDPs — Vulnerability Disclosure Programs)
    • Focus on one vulnerability class at a time
    • Read disclosed bug bounty reports on HackerOne for inspiration

    19. Ethics & Legal Framework¶

    The Law¶

    • Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — US law; unauthorized access is criminal
    • Computer Misuse Act (1990) — UK equivalent
    • GDPR — EU privacy regulation affecting security professionals
    • HIPAA — US healthcare data law
    • PCI-DSS — Payment card industry standard

    Always Get Written Authorization¶

    Before any security testing: - Obtain signed Rules of Engagement (RoE) - Define scope clearly (IPs, domains, excluded systems) - Define timing and emergency contact procedures - Understand what "success" looks like and reporting obligations

    Responsible Disclosure¶

    If you find a vulnerability outside of a bug bounty program: 1. Don't exploit it beyond proof of concept 2. Don't disclose publicly before the vendor can fix it 3. Contact the organization's security team (check for security@ email or security.txt) 4. Give reasonable time to patch (90 days is industry standard — Google Project Zero) 5. Coordinate disclosure timing

    Ethics Code¶

    • Never attack systems without explicit permission
    • Protect confidential data you encounter during testing
    • Don't share client data or vulnerabilities with third parties
    • Act in the best interest of the people whose systems you protect
    • Report ALL findings, not just the impressive ones

    21. Burnout & Mental Health¶

    Cybersecurity can be intensely demanding, especially in incident response, SOC, and red team roles.

    Common Stressors¶

    • On-call and after-hours incidents — breaches don't respect business hours
    • High-stakes decisions — errors have real consequences
    • Adversarial mindset 24/7 — always thinking about what can go wrong
    • Under-resourced teams — too much to protect, too few people
    • Imposter syndrome — the field is vast and evolves fast

    Warning Signs¶

    • Constant fatigue despite sleeping
    • Cynicism about your work or organization
    • Difficulty concentrating
    • Feeling like your work doesn't matter
    • Physical symptoms (headaches, tension)

    Healthy Practices¶

    • Set firm boundaries on after-hours work when possible
    • Rotate on-call responsibilities fairly within teams
    • Take real vacations — completely disconnected
    • Exercise and physical activity — essential for mental resilience
    • Community — connect with other security professionals
    • Pursue interests outside security — hobbies matter
    • Seek professional support if needed — many countries have EAP programs

    Industry Culture Note¶

    The "hacker" culture can sometimes glorify overwork and "hustling." Sustainable careers are built on balance, not burnout. Senior practitioners consistently advise: it's a marathon, not a sprint.
